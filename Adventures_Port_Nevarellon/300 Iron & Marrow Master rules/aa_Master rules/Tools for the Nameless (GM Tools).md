# Economic Baselines

- Currency Standard: The primary day-to-day trade currency is the Silver Piece (sp). Copper Pennies (cp) are used by peasants (10 cp = 1 sp). Gold Sovereigns (gs) are held only by nobility and wealthy cartels (1 gs = 20 sp).
    
- Item Degradation: Items can be Damaged (reduces effectiveness or adds a flaw) or Ruined (useless until repaired via a Hammer & Forge downtime action).

# Targeting the Resources

Because the community supply die is a tangible mechanic, the GM can use their Threat pool to attack the party's supplies instead of their health, creating terrifying new enemy archetypes.

- The Rust Monster / Acid Spit: If an enemy with a corrosive or fire-based attack wins a Clash with a high margin, the GM can spend 1 Threat to force an immediate Supply Die roll as the party's gear melts or catches fire.
    
- The Scavenger: Small, fodder-tier enemies (like goblins or feral ghouls) might have a rule where if they win a Clash, they don't deal Impact. Instead, they slice open a backpack and flee, forcing an automatic step-down of the Supply Die without a roll.

Non-weapon damage needs to fall into one of three distinct, terrifying categories:

### 1. Direct Stress (The Attrition Engine)

For minor environmental hazards, toxic environments, or persistent conditions like fire. It does not instantly sever a limb or crush bone, but the pain and exhaustion rapidly accelerate the Death Spiral.

- The Mechanic: Bypasses the Wound Threshold completely and deals Direct Dissonant Stress.
    
- Example Revision (Ablaze): "At the start of your turn, the agonizing heat causes you to suffer 2 Dissonant Stress before you can act."
    

### 2. Direct Wounds (The Lethal Bypass)

For catastrophic hazards, unmitigated magic, or Boss abilities that are narratively designed to rend flesh and ignore armor.

- The Mechanic: Bypasses the Wound Threshold completely and automatically crosses out 1 Wound Slot.
    
- Example Revision (Gehenna's Grip): "Failure means they are violently dragged 10 feet toward Malaphar. The searing chains melt through their armor, inflicting 1 Direct Wound."
    

### 3. Hazard Rolls (The Trap Mechanic)

When a falling boulder, explosive trap, or massive environmental collapse occurs, it shouldn't just be a flat number. It should strike the player like an enemy would, testing their armor and resolve.

- The Mechanic: The GM rolls a 2d6 + Hazard Power to generate a massive Impact total, which is then compared against the player's Wound Threshold just like a sword swing.
    
- Example: A collapsing ceiling trap rolls 2d6 + 4. It totals a 14. Because 14 is higher than the Fighter's Wound Threshold of 10, the Fighter takes a Wound. If it rolled an 8, the armor holds, and the Fighter only takes 1 Stress.

In Iron & Marrow, monsters do not challenge the players by having bigger numbers. They challenge the players by breaking the rules of the game.

This is managed through the GM's Threat Economy and a tiered Bestiary Tag system. Here is the framework for designing brutal, terrifying encounters.

### 1. The Threat Economy (The GM's Momentum)

Threat is the GM’s equivalent of the players' Momentum. It is a shared pool of points used to activate horrifying monster abilities, interrupt the action economy, and aggressively push the players into the Death Spiral.

- The Starting Pool: The GM begins an encounter with a base Threat pool equal to the number of Elite or Boss enemies on the field (minimum 1).
    
- Generating Threat: The GM does not gain Threat by rolling well. Threat is generated by the bloody reality of the fight:
    

	- Blood Scent: Whenever an Elite or Boss suffers a physical Wound, the GM instantly gains 1 Threat.
    
	- Player Catastrophe: If a player rolls a Natural 2 (Double 1s) on any check, the GM instantly gains 1 Threat.
    
	- The Escalation Die: At the top of every new combat round, the GM automatically gains 1 Threat (representing the closing walls of stamina and time).
    

- The Hard Cap: To prevent the GM from hoarding points and wiping the party in a single turn, the Threat pool is hard-capped at 5.
    

### 2. The Three Tiers of Monsters

Because modifiers are bounded, monsters are categorized by how they interact with the game's action economy and the Threat pool.

Tier 1: Fodder (The Swarm)

Goblins, feral hounds, decaying thralls.

- The Math: +1 to +3 Modifiers. Wound Threshold: 7 or 8. Stress Limit: 0 (They die or flee immediately if they take physical trauma or high Dissonant Stress).
    
- The Role: Fodder do not use the Threat pool. Their entire purpose is to drain player Momentum, force the party to waste Aggressor actions, and trigger the Swarm Bonus (if two or more Fodder engage a single player, that player loses the ability to Dodge).
    

Tier 2: Elites (The Wall)

Orc Warlords, heavily armored Constructs, Cultist Priests.

- The Math: +4 to +6 Modifiers. Wound Threshold: 10. Stress Limit: 3.
    
- The Role: The bread and butter of the tactical engine. Elites have access to the GM's Threat pool to fuel specific, devastating abilities (like Ugarth's Bone Breaker). They are sticky, lethal, and require the players to spend Momentum to systematically dismantle them.
    

Tier 3: Bosses (The Paradigm Breakers)

Ancient Dragons, Void Demons, Corrupted Kings.

- The Math: +7 to +8 Modifiers (they are the only entities allowed to break the +5 mortal cap). Wound Threshold: 12 to 14. Stress Limit: 5+.
    
- The Role: A Boss does not just fight the party; it dictates the environment. Bosses take two full Aggressor turns per round. Furthermore, they usually possess a passive Threat Generator (e.g., Aura of Terror: The GM generates 1 Threat every time a player spends Momentum).
    

### 3. Bestiary Tags (Spending Threat to Break Rules)

Instead of giving a monster a unique, paragraph-long spell description, you assign them universal Tags. When the GM spends Threat, these Tags activate, violently shifting the tactical grid.

- Cost 1: Cleave (Spatial Threat)
    

	- The Trigger: Declared after winning an Aggressor Clash.
    
	- The Effect: The monster's Impact is not applied to a single target. It is applied to the primary target, and any other player standing in the monster's frontal arc. Forces the party to manage their spacing.
    

- Cost 1: Corrosive/Cursed Blood (Attrition Threat)
    

	- The Trigger: Declared when the monster takes a physical Wound.
    
	- The Effect: The monster bleeds toxic or void-touched ichor. The player who delivered the Wound instantly suffers 1 Dissonant Stress, or their weapon gains the Damaged tag.
    

- Cost 2: Unstoppable Mass (Action Economy Threat)
    

	- The Trigger: Declared when the monster loses a Clash and is about to take a massive Wound.
    
	- The Effect: The monster completely ignores the physical Impact of the blow. However, the exertion costs them their entire next Aggressor action.
    

- Cost 3: Lair Action / Geometry Shift (Environmental Threat)
    

	- The Trigger: Declared at the absolute start of a combat round.
    
	- The Effect: The monster alters the physical map. A Dragon spends 3 Threat to flood the room with waist-deep water (Mire condition for the whole party). A Necromancer spends 3 Threat to immediately summon 1d4 Fodder skeletons from the dirt.

By building your Bestiary this way, creating a new monster takes seconds. You assign them a Tier, give them a reasonable modifier (+4), pick their Wound Threshold (10), and slap two terrifying Tags on their sheet. The Threat economy naturally balances the fight, ensuring the GM can only trigger the worst abilities when the encounter has truly escalated.
